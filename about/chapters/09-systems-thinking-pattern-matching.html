09.
Systems Thinking & Pattern Matching ​

"Vision paints the picture of what we want to create. Systems thinking reveals how we have created what we currently have."

The Fifth Discipline, Peter Senge

Recognizing Archetypes, Developing Instinct ​

We tend to overvalue words and overlook behavior. Words tend to have too much power over us, muddling our ability to understand people's choices. When someone disappoints us we want to chalk it up to their being a victim of their circumstances rather than looking for behavioral patterns: a repeating habit that would challenge us to make braver decisions of our own. We reassure ourselves that we're measuring the performance of our expectations against our results — generously handing out KPIs, OKRs, SMART goals, etc. — but in the cold light of day we're just going through the motions.

Let's remove the burden of cooking up a recipe of goals we hope will steer complex improvements in our people. We can do better by keeping things simpler.

Measure Behaviors, Not Words ​

By making our expectations crystal clear it's fair to judge behaviors which either satisfy what we want (or fail to do so). Some examples to illustrate what I mean:

  • I expect you to be proactive, to use your time wisely, and bring only problems to me which you've tried to solve but cannot
  • I want to see people helping each other, not covering their asses. If you're spending time defending yourself you're wasting time by not helping your team
  • Good ideas can come from anywhere; I expect you all to contribute, not to wait for me to decide

More than any Key Performance Indicator, these measures (of behavior) will tell you exactly who 'gets it' and who will struggle to last. Measuring performance is not supposed to be a Hunger Games-style game show designed to sort the weak from the strong, it's meant to measure professional growth. Overly complicated job descriptions that list every possible responsibility under the sun begin the owner-operator's confusing struggle. Overly complicated measures of success continue that chore, burdening management with surveiling their own people instead of providing essential, honest feedback employees can immediately use for their own growth.

"The problem was [...] the tendency of managers to confront complex business issues with '9-point programs,' as if the problem is fixing a flat tire."

The Fifth Discipline, Peter Senge

It's dishonest to ignore how complex work has become, but simple approaches to complex problems need not be reductive.

Kill Fake Urgency ​

Patrick Lencioni was wrong when he said: "If everything is important nothing is important," and the reason matters. Let's accept two things about "important" and "urgent" in the modern workplace:

  1. Every task is important (we wouldn't do it if it wasn't)
  2. Not every task is urgent (some things can wait, others cannot wait)

We cannot get everything done urgently, so it's vital to triage what needs to happen immediately and what comes after what's most urgent. Everything being important, we need to avoid fake urgency that feels like we're getting something done when we're really just spinning our wheels without any real traction. "If everything is important urgent nothing is important urgent."

Stop Reacting, Start Creating ​

"The dominance of the 'event mentality' tells people that the name of the game is reacting to change, not generating change."

The Fifth Discipline, Peter Senge

There are too many reasons to list why small companies find themselves in a reactionary mindset on the average workday. In most cases it begins at the point of sale, where a prospect, seeking reassurance, looks you in the eye and asks you if they're going to regret their purchase. Payroll, never being more than two weeks away, looms large in your mind as you say we will move heaven and earth to make your dreams come true. It is absolutely natural to want to satisfy your new client. Besides, no one on your team knows the full extent of your promises, so what's the harm? Fast forward six months and:

  • Fingers are pointed on both sides about why the project is late
  • Only half of the work has been done; all the budget is spent
  • Relationships inside and out are strained

What's the alternative? Systems thinking. Pattern matching. Introspection. Honesty. That sounds great, what do those things look and feel like?

  • When people doing the work tell you they feel uneasy about the way things are going, ask them to elaborate
  • When the client misses a meeting, label it as a problem with the client
  • When the client is unhappy with our work, create space and time to talk through internally whether this was an avoidable, complex, or intelligent mistake; don't assume any single point of failure
  • When someone on our team falls below our standard — cameras on; good lighting; a positive demeanor is mandatory — we address that head on, immediately, with the individual (especially if that's the CEO)
  • Meetings used to be much snappier and useful. They've slipped into deep-dives on frustrating clients. We need to address the slippage and own the failure

Operating values are more useful than core values because you can point at them — they're naked. A core value of 'be the change' and an operating value of 'give feedback immediately' may have similar intent but one is simpler to practice and measure.

Angry People Make Bad Decisions ​

We're taught from a young age that motivated people look a certain way. Usually it's a state of agitation, of uprightness, of wide-eyed decisiveness. We expect highly motivated people to rise early, to have no sense of humor, to be disinterested in food, to work unreasonable hours, keep themselves in good shape, to label themselves "Type A." All that "on"ness comes at a cost.

These people invest heavily in what Daniel Kahneman calls System 2.

An example of S1 (System 1) and (System 2):

S1 is what you use to get from A to B over a 30-minute car journey. You hardly seem to use your brain to make the hundreds (or thousands) of decisions or judgments over that half-hour. S2 is the conversation you have with your spouse while driving.

S1 is used when:

  • Recognizing the smell of cut grass
  • Finding the next word in the phrase "Jack and Jill went up the..."
  • Unscrewing a bottle lid

S2 is used when:

  • Calculating (in your mind) the result of 1,482 minus 967

  • Explaining the difference between the words "further" and "farther"

  • Remaining calm and helpful when dealing with a rude customer

  • S1 is cheap, fast, and biased

  • S2 is expensive, slow, and lazy

These Type A folks love S2. They tend to distrust anything that resembles instinct or pattern-matching (S1). The bad news is that you live in their world — we all live in a world where S1 is constantly dismissed in favor of S2. The good news is that by embracing S1 you can leverage hundreds of thousands of years of evolution that comes with every human brain, yours included. All you need to train your S1 is time, patience, and calm. Not adjectives we commonly use to describe modern capitalism. Which is why it's crucial to prioritize openness, a dedication to learning, and acceptance that progress requires change.

The alternative is to base your operation on S2. Most do. Which is why burnout is endemic. Not because S2 is bad nor vital (in any organization), but because we've evolved to observe reality and move the way we operate from S2 — think of a toddler trying to put a square block into a star-shaped hole — to S1 — when was the last time you needed to focus when using a door handle? We need to move conscious effort (S2) to effortlessly unconscious (S1) in order to spend the little remaining energy we have on what demands our slow, concentrated calculation. If you have to use more of S2 than S1 in a given day, you will fall asleep earlier and take longer to recover.

It's very difficult to survive using only one of these two systems. The race for attention in modern capitalism has come at the expense of your needing to use S2 to the extreme. That imbalance needs addressing by the owners of small businesses who truly want functional, healthy employees whose commitment is even higher than their "Type A" counterparts. You want calm, confident employees whose instincts you trust to steer the operation in the right direction each day.

Thinking in systems (facing the reality of what's happening in your company) and patten matching (what are the underlying, repeating patterns of behavior that characterize our identity as a group) are foundational tools, both requiring time and patience to comprehend their vital importance.

Kahneman's System 1 vs System 2: Goal is to develop System 1 pattern matching through experience so you're not deep-diving constantly. This is mastery.

"The dominance of conclusions over arguments is most pronounced where emotions are involved."

Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow

Senge's systems thinking applied to daily professional work. Most situations are 5-6 recurring patterns. Ability to recognize which archetype you're in, predict consequences, short-circuit for best fit.

Why AI can follow rules but can't improvise when patterns break. Human judgment developed through safe failure is irreplaceable. Small firms are messy because of the noise to signal ratio. There's too much noise to concentrate, and yet our best work happens when we can focus. Insisting on your business being a learning company is the only way to rationalize the noise, to make sense of signals, and to learn from (inevitable) mistakes.

"The art of progress is to preserve order amid change, and to preserve change amid order."

— Alfred North Whitehead
